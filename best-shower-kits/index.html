Know the field

Almost every shower kit sold in the United States falls into one of these six groups. They are not all competing for the same job, which is why "best" only makes sense once you know which group you are in.

Where the cheap kits actually work

A $600 three-piece alcove kit from a home center is not a scam. For a rental unit, a basement bathroom, or a shower nobody uses daily, it is the right call. The panels are thin, the gelcoat is thin, and the floor flexes underfoot, but it holds water and it costs almost nothing.

The problem starts when that same kit gets sold into a primary bathroom for someone who is 74 years old. The curb is four to six inches tall. The walls will not hold a grab bar without adding blocking behind them. And the floor is the part that gives out first.

If price is the only constraint, buy the cheap kit and spend the savings on a proper grab bar anchored into framing. That is an honest answer, and it is the one we give on the phone when it is true.

Where a built-to-order kit earns its price

Three things change when a kit is made after you order it rather than before.

First, it fits. A 58″ opening does not need a 60″ kit plus filler strips and a caulk line you will be redoing in two years. It needs a 58″ unit.

Second, the walls are backed. Full wood reinforcement behind the wall panels means a grab bar, a fold-down seat or a handheld glide bar can be mounted anywhere, later, without opening the wall.

Third, the entry height is a choice instead of a leftover. You pick half an inch, or an inch, or two. That single decision is the difference between a shower somebody uses and a shower somebody is afraid of.

Method

How we judge a shower kit

Seven checks. Ask any supplier these and you will learn more in five minutes than an hour of reading reviews.

  1. Check 01

    What is behind the wall panel?

    Ask whether the walls are reinforced, and with what. Laminated wood backing means a grab bar can be screwed anywhere. A hollow panel means you need blocking in the framing before the shower goes in, and you need to decide grab bar positions now, permanently.

  2. Check 02

    How tall is the entry, exactly?

    Not "low threshold." A number. Half an inch, one inch, two inches. Vagueness here is the single biggest tell that a kit was designed to a price rather than to a use.

  3. Check 03

    Is the pan pre-sloped and pre-leveled?

    A pan that needs a mortar bed adds a day of labor and a failure point. A pan that arrives sloped to the drain from the factory does not.

  4. Check 04

    What does the floor feel like underfoot?

    Stand in the display unit. If it flexes or drums, that is thin laminate over too little structure. It will craze and crack near the drain first.

  5. Check 05

    What is the warranty, and on what?

    Read whether it covers the material, the finish, or just manufacturing defects. Ours is a 30-year limited warranty against manufacturing defects on the fiberglass-reinforced showers, walls and pans, from the date of installation.

  6. Check 06

    Can it change later?

    Most people buying a shower in their fifties will still be using it in their seventies. Ask whether a seat, a second grab bar or a handheld can be added in year eight without a remodel.

  7. Check 07

    Will it physically get to the bathroom?

    A one-piece unit is stronger and seamless. It is also five feet wide and rigid. Measure the narrowest turn in the path from your front door to the bathroom before anyone quotes you a one-piece.

The cost nobody quotes you

Angi's 2026 figures put a professionally installed walk-in shower at an average near $9,000, with most jobs landing between $6,000 and $12,000. A tub-to-shower conversion is wider still, from around $1,200 to $8,000.

The kit is rarely what moves that number. Labor and plumbing do.

Two changes blow up a budget faster than any product choice. Moving the drain, and cutting a slab. If the existing drain does not line up with the new pan, rough-in plumbing can jump from a few hundred dollars to several thousand. If the shower is on a concrete slab and the design needs a recessed drain, someone is renting a saw.

This is exactly why the above-floor-rough kit exists, and why we ask about your floor structure on the first call rather than the third.

What this means for you

Before you compare kit prices, find out where your drain is and what your floor is made of. A $900 kit that forces a $3,000 plumbing change is more expensive than a $2,600 kit that does not.

The one spec that matters

Threshold height decides everything else

Pick this number first. Size, drain, wall layout and price all follow from it.

Entry heights, drawn to scale

A standard bathtub wall is 14 to 16 inches. That is the step most people are replacing.

How to choose the number

Half an inch. The ADA maximum for a roll-in shower compartment. Choose it if a wheelchair rolls in, if a caregiver pushes a shower chair, or if you want the entry to stop being a consideration at all. It asks more of the floor, because the drain has to sit lower or run in a trench.

One inch. The quiet favorite. Almost nobody trips on an inch, water stays in reliably, and the floor work stays simple. If you are buying for a future you cannot predict, this is the safe middle.

Two inches. Fine for a steady adult, and the easiest to install into an existing bathroom. Note that the ADA only permits a two-inch threshold in a transfer-type compartment in an existing facility, and only where a half-inch would disturb the structural reinforcement of the floor slab. For a private home, it is simply a choice.

Four inches. A traditional curb. It keeps water in with zero effort and it is the cheapest to build. It is also a step, and steps are what the CDC data is about.

Rule of thumb

If you are over 60, or buying for a parent, or this is the only shower in the house, go lower than feels necessary. Nobody has ever called us to say the threshold was too low.

An honest note about fiberglass

Plenty of remodelers say fiberglass gelcoat dulls in five to seven years. On the thin panels you find in a flat-pack kit, that is fair. It is not a description of a heavy-gelcoat, wood-reinforced unit built over a barrier coat and warranted for thirty years. The word "fiberglass" covers both, and that is exactly why it is a bad way to shop. Compare gelcoat thickness, wall reinforcement and warranty instead.

Measure it in three numbers

  1. Width, wall to wall. Measure at the floor, at hip height and at the top. Old houses are rarely square. Give us all three.
  2. Depth, front to back. Same rule. Measure at the floor first, because that is where the pan sits.
  3. Height to the ceiling. Panels are usually 72″ to 84″ tall. Low ceilings and soffits change the wall layout.

Then find the drain. Stand at the opening and note whether it is left, right or center, and roughly how far in from the front edge.

That is enough for us to quote. Everything else we can sort out on the phone.

If the numbers do not match a stock size

That is normal and it is not a problem. About a third of what we ship is built to a dimension that is not on any list. It adds cost, but far less than most people assume.

Avoid these

Five mistakes we see every week

1. Buying the shower before checking the drain

The drain position in the old tub and the drain position in the new pan are not the same thing. A tub drain is usually at one end, close to the wall, on a 1.5″ line. Many shower pans, especially linear-drain designs, want a 2″ line. Find this out before you buy, not on install day.

2. Treating grab bars as an afterthought

Grab bars get added later in almost every case. If the walls are not reinforced, "later" means opening the wall. Buy the reinforced walls even if you do not want bars today.

3. Choosing a one-piece unit that cannot get inside

One-piece showers are excellent in new construction, where the walls go up after the unit goes in. In a remodel they frequently will not turn the corner at the top of a staircase. Measure the path.

4. Picking size before picking threshold

People fall in love with a footprint, then discover the entry height they need forces a different pan, a different drain and a different price. Threshold first. Always.

5. Assuming "ADA" on a product page means compliant

ADA compliance is about the finished compartment, not the box it came in. A roll-in compartment needs a minimum of 30 × 60 clear inside, a 60″ entry, and a 30 × 60 clear floor space next to it. A transfer compartment needs 36 × 36 clear inside with a 36″ entry and a seat 17 to 19 inches off the floor. A compliant pan installed in a non-compliant space is not compliant.

Questions people actually ask

What is the best shower kit for an older adult?

A barrier-free or low-threshold kit with reinforced walls, a slip-resistant floor, and room for a seat. In practice that means a half-inch or one-inch entry, a footprint of at least 36 by 36 inches, and wall panels that will take a grab bar anywhere. Threshold height matters more than brand, finish or price. See shower kits for seniors.

Are expensive shower kits actually better?

Above roughly $2,000 you are usually paying for structure rather than looks: thicker laminate, wood-backed walls, a heavier pan, and the ability to build to your dimension. Below roughly $1,000 you are paying for a waterproof box. Between the two, read the spec sheet carefully, because that band contains both good value and badly disguised thin panels.

Can a shower kit replace a bathtub?

Yes, and it is the most common job we quote. A standard tub alcove is 60 by 30 or 60 by 32 inches, which is exactly why those kit sizes exist. The work involves removing the tub, adjusting the drain, and sometimes patching the wall above. Most conversions take one to three days. See tub-to-shower conversion.

How long does a shower kit last?

A thin big-box kit typically looks tired in seven to ten years and the floor is usually the first thing to go. A reinforced unit with a heavy gelcoat over a barrier coat is a different product; ours carry a 30-year limited warranty against manufacturing defects from the date of installation. Cleaning matters either way. Abrasive powders and bleach-heavy cleaners shorten the life of any gelcoat surface.

Is a one-piece or multi-piece kit better?

One-piece has no seams, so it is the stronger choice when the space allows it, which normally means new construction or a gut remodel with an open wall. Multi-piece is better for almost every retrofit, because the panels fit through a doorway and up a staircase. Multi-piece units with a locking wall joint seal reliably; the seam is not the weak point people assume it is.

Can I install a shower kit myself?

Setting the pan and hanging the panels is within reach of a confident DIYer. The plumbing usually is not, and in most jurisdictions moving a drain requires a permit and an inspection. Our usual advice is to do the demolition yourself if you want to save money, and pay a plumber for the drain connection. See the installation guide.

What is the difference between a shower kit and a shower pan?

A pan is the floor only. A kit is the pan plus the wall panels, and usually the trim and drain. People buy a pan alone when they want tile on the walls but a reliable, pre-sloped, factory-made floor underneath. That combination is common and it works well. See shower pans.

Do you ship nationwide?

Yes. Kits ship by freight across the continental United States. Lead time on most kits runs about a week and a half, and selected in-stock configurations ship the next business day. Certain valve and fixture brands take longer, and we will tell you before you order rather than after.

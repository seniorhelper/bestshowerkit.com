The short answer

Barrier-free means the raised curb is gone. In its place is a threshold between 0.5″ and 2″ tall, low enough to step over without lifting your knee, and at the bottom of that range low enough to roll across.

Only the 0.5″ threshold meets the ADA roll-in maximum. Everything above it is a low-threshold shower. That distinction matters enormously on a code-driven project and hardly at all in a private home, and we will be precise about which situation you are in.

Most residential customers land on 0.75″ or 1″. It gives nearly all of the benefit with far less floor work, because a 0.5″ pan usually has to be recessed into the framing.

Everything ships with reinforced wall backing throughout, so grab bars go where the person needs them rather than where a stud happens to be.

Language

Four words that get used as if they mean the same thing.

They do not, and the differences carry real consequences on a code-driven job. Here is what each one actually means before we go any further.

Why be this pedantic? Because we have spoken to too many people who bought a shower advertised as "ADA compliant" that failed an inspection, or who paid for floor recessing they never actually needed. Getting the words right saves both of those outcomes.

Beveled or traditional edge

Every height comes in both. A beveled edge ramps gently and is kinder to walker wheels, rolling chair casters and bare toes. A traditional edge is squarer and holds water back marginally better. If mobility is the reason you are here, choose beveled.

ADA & code

Straight answers about what actually complies.

A great deal of shower marketing calls anything with a low step "ADA compliant." That is not how the standard works, and on a commercial project it is the kind of claim that fails an inspection. Here are the real numbers.

Transfer shower

Exactly 36″×36″ clear inside, measured at the center points of opposing sides, with a 36″ minimum clear entry. For someone who moves from a wheelchair onto a seat mounted 17″–19″ above the floor. The standard allows no construction tolerance on that dimension.

Standard roll-in

Minimum 30″×60″ clear inside, with a 60″ wide entry along the full open face, plus a 30″×60″ clear floor space beside the opening. Some jurisdictions ask for 36″ depth so a caregiver can assist.

Alternate roll-in

Minimum 36″×60″ clear inside, with a 36″ wide entry at one end of the long side. The short return wall gives you somewhere to mount a seat.

The part most suppliers leave out

The ADA caps a roll-in shower threshold at 0.5″. Only our 0.5″ pans meet that requirement. A 1″ or 1.5″ unit is a low-threshold shower — a genuinely excellent product, considerably safer than a curb, and the right answer for most homes. It is not an ADA roll-in threshold, and we will not tell you it is.

One narrow exception exists: a threshold up to 2″ is permitted in transfer-type compartments in existing facilities, and only where fitting a 0.5″ threshold would disturb the structural reinforcement of the floor slab.

Two more things worth knowing. A curbless shower is not automatically compliant — compliance depends on interior clear dimensions, controls, grab bars, spray placement and the clear floor space outside the compartment. And the ADA is federal law: your state or city may require more, though never less. On any commercial or code-driven project, confirm the layout with your local building department before ordering. We will send spec sheets and submittals to help that conversation.

Which type

Roll-in, transfer, or simply low.

Three different products for three different situations. Getting this right at the start saves an expensive correction later, so it is worth being honest about how the shower will be used on an ordinary Tuesday.

Roll-in

Entered while seated in a wheelchair or rolling shower chair. The user does not transfer at all, which matters when transferring is difficult or a caregiver is assisting.

  • Threshold 0.5″
  • Minimum 30×60 clear inside
  • Wide open entry
  • Usually needs the pan recessed

Transfer

For someone who can move from a wheelchair onto a seat. Compact, which makes it the practical answer where floor space is genuinely tight.

  • Exactly 36×36 clear inside
  • Seat at 17″–19″
  • Grab bars on back and side
  • Smallest compliant footprint

Low threshold

The one most people actually need. Nobody uses a wheelchair, but stepping over a curb has stopped feeling automatic. This is the aging-in-place shower.

  • Threshold 0.75″–2″
  • Any of 22 stock sizes
  • Minimal floor work
  • Lowest total cost

Drainage

Where the water goes matters more when there is no curb.

A curbed shower forgives a lot. A barrier-free shower has almost nothing holding water in, so the drain choice does real work. Here is how the three options behave.

Traditional round drain

Center, left or right. Matches most existing waste lines, which is why it is the cheapest option to install. Pair it with a water stopper on a barrier-free pan.

Front trench drain

Catches water at the entry, exactly where a barrier-free shower is most likely to let it escape. Drains faster and lets the floor slope in one clean direction. Our usual recommendation for roll-in work.

Trench threshold

Drainage and containment become one component, at 1″ or 2″. No separate lip to catch a walker wheel or a caster.

The water stopper rule

Barrier-free pan plus a traditional round drain? Add a water stopper. It is a removable dam that lies across the entry, and it exists to solve the one genuine drawback of a very low threshold: with almost nothing holding water in and a single round outlet to travel to, a small amount can find its way onto the bathroom floor.

It sits flush, it lifts straight out when somebody needs to roll in, and it costs very little. One is included in our accessory package.

With a front trench drain it matters much less, because the trench is already catching water right where it would otherwise escape. That is the main reason we push people toward trench drains on true roll-in installations. Read more on the shower pans page.

The details that matter

A low threshold alone is not an accessible shower.

Removing the curb is the headline. What actually determines whether someone can use this shower independently, day after day, is the fittings around it.

  • Full reinforced wall backing. This is the one we would not compromise on. Backing runs throughout the panels, so grab bars mount at the height that suits the person rather than wherever a stud lands — and they can be moved if needs change.
  • A handheld wand on a slider bar. A 5′ hose on an adjustable bar is the single most useful fitting in any accessible shower. It works seated, standing, and for anyone assisting.
  • A fold-down seat. A 22×15″ padded seat that folds flat when nobody needs it. Not a medical device, just somewhere to sit. Our built-in vs fold-down seat comparison explains when each one makes sense.
  • Two stainless grab bars. One at the entry, one inside. Where exactly depends on the person, which is what the reinforced backing is for.
  • A slip-resistant floor. The texture is moulded into the pan surface, not painted on, so it does not wear away with cleaning.
  • Controls you can reach. A four-port mixing valve in Delta or Moen, positioned so it can be operated from the seat as well as standing.

What this is really about

A shower is not usually the reason someone leaves their home. It is often the last straw.

People rarely move into care because of one big event. It is an accumulation of small daily things that stopped being manageable, and washing is very often near the top of that list.

The bathroom is where independence gets tested twice a day, in private, with nobody there to help. When it starts going badly, people cope by washing less, or by accepting help they did not want to need.

A shower somebody can walk or roll into, with a bar to hold and a seat when standing is too much, quietly removes that problem. It is a small piece of equipment against a large question.

We are not going to pretend a shower kit solves ageing. But of the things you can buy to help someone stay in their own house, it is among the most effective per dollar spent.

What is a barrier-free shower?

A barrier-free shower has a very low threshold in place of a raised curb, so there is little or nothing to step over. Ours run from 0.5 inches to 2 inches in quarter-inch increments, in beveled or traditional edge profiles. They suit anyone with balance concerns, anyone using a walker or a cane, wheelchair users, and anyone who would simply rather not climb into the shower every morning.

Is a barrier-free shower the same as ADA compliant?

No, and the difference matters. Barrier-free is a product description covering our whole 0.5 to 2 inch range. ADA compliance is a legal standard. The ADA caps a roll-in shower threshold at 0.5 inches, so only our 0.5 inch pans meet that requirement. Compliance also depends on interior clear dimensions, grab bars, controls, spray placement and the clear floor space outside the compartment, not just the threshold.

What threshold heights do you make?

0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.25, 1.5, 1.75 and 2 inches, in quarter-inch steps, each available with a beveled or traditional edge. Most suppliers offer two or three. We build seven because real bathrooms have floor build-ups, tile thicknesses and joist depths that do not land on round numbers.

Which threshold height should I choose?

Choose 0.5 inches if a wheelchair or rolling shower chair is involved, or if the project is code-driven, but budget for recessing the pan into the floor framing. Choose 0.75 to 1 inch for most homes, which is where the majority of our residential customers land: nearly all the accessibility benefit with very little floor work. Choose 1.5 to 2 inches if water containment matters more than the last half inch of step height.

What is the difference between a roll-in and a transfer shower?

A roll-in shower is entered while seated in a wheelchair or rolling chair, with no transfer at all. Under the ADA a standard roll-in compartment needs at least 30 by 60 inches clear inside with a 60 inch wide entry, or 36 by 60 inches in the alternate layout. A transfer shower is for someone who moves from a wheelchair onto a seat, and must measure exactly 36 by 36 inches clear inside with a 36 inch entry and a seat 17 to 19 inches above the floor.

Does a barrier-free shower leak?

Not if it is specified properly. The pan is one piece with a non-split floor and a slope built in, so the water has somewhere to go. The consideration is the entry: with almost nothing holding water back, a small amount can escape. Two things address it. A front trench drain catches water at the entry, which is why we recommend it for roll-in installations. Or, with a traditional round drain, a removable water stopper laid across the entry does the same job for very little money.

What is a water stopper?

A removable dam that sits across the entry of a barrier-free shower. It lies flush, lifts straight out when someone needs to roll in, and holds back the small amount of water that a very low threshold cannot. We recommend one whenever a barrier-free pan is paired with a traditional round drain, and one is included in our accessory package.

Does a 0.5 inch pan need to be recessed into the floor?

Usually, yes. To bring the top of the threshold within half an inch of your finished floor, the pan generally has to sit down into the floor framing. On a timber floor that means cutting and reinforcing between joists, and typically adds $400 to $1,200 in labour. On a concrete slab it means cutting the slab and costs considerably more. If recessing is not practical, a 0.75 inch threshold with a beveled edge delivers most of the same daily benefit.

What sizes do barrier-free showers come in?

Nineteen multi-piece footprints from 36x36 up to 72x48 inches, plus three single-piece configurations at 48x36, 60x30 and 60x33. Barrier-free pans on their own come in twenty sizes up to 78x48. Custom dimensions are built to order for openings that match nothing standard.

What size is best for aging in place?

60 by 36 inches is our usual recommendation for a home. The extra depth over a 60x32 is what makes room for a fold-down seat and for someone to assist, and it still fits a standard bathtub opening in most houses. For a code-driven roll-in installation, 60x30 is the ADA standard minimum.

Can grab bars be fitted anywhere?

Yes, and this is one of the most useful things about the system. Full reinforced backing runs throughout the wall panels, so bars mount at the height and position that suits the person using the shower rather than wherever a stud happens to fall. If someone's needs change later, the wall is already prepared and the bars can be repositioned.

Are barrier-free showers slippery?

The floor has a slip-resistant texture moulded into the pan surface rather than a coating applied on top, so it does not wear away with cleaning. Combined with grab bars and a fold-down seat, that addresses most of what makes a shower risky. No surface is a substitute for something to hold onto, which is why we treat grab bars as essential rather than optional.

Can I convert my bathtub to a barrier-free shower?

Yes, and it is one of the most common reasons people call. A standard alcove tub is 60 inches long, so a 60 inch barrier-free pan usually drops into the footprint the tub leaves behind. The thing to watch is drain position, since tub drains sit at one end rather than the center. See our tub to shower conversion page for the full process.

Will a barrier-free shower fit through my bathroom door?

Multi-piece units are designed to. Do I need a permit for a barrier-free shower?

A like-for-like replacement that does not move plumbing often needs no permit. Recessing a pan, relocating a drain or altering framing usually does. Requirements vary by city and county. On any commercial or code-driven project, confirm the layout with your local building department before ordering, and we will supply spec sheets and submittals to support that conversation.

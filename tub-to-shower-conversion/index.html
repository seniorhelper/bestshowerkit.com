Tub to shower

Turn that bathtub into a shower you actually use.

Most bathtubs in this country get used as showers. They just come with a 15″–19″ porcelain wall in front of them. Take the tub out, drop a walk-in shower into the same footprint, and the wall goes away for good.

The short answer

A standard alcove bathtub measures 60″ long. That is the whole reason this project is usually simple. A 60″ wide shower pan drops into the space the tub leaves behind, so the walls stay where they are and nobody touches your framing. Manufactured homes are the common exception, with tub spaces that are often smaller; our guide to shower kits for mobile and manufactured homes covers what to check.

The depth is what varies. Measure from the finished back wall to the front edge of the tub, and that number points you to 60×30, 60×32, 60×34 or 60×36. If it lands between two sizes, we build the pan to your exact dimension instead.

Budget $4,500–$8,500 all in with professional installation. Most of the spread comes down to one thing: whether the new drain lines up with your existing waste line.

And before you commit, read the section on resale below. There is one rule worth knowing, and we would rather you heard it from us than from a buyer's agent in three years.

The daily reality

The tub stopped being a bath a long time ago.

Be honest about how the room actually gets used. For most households, the tub in the main bathroom is where the shower happens. It holds the shampoo. Occasionally it holds the dog. Almost nobody soaks in it.

Meanwhile it asks something of you every single morning. A standard tub wall stands roughly 15″ to 19″ tall. Lifting one leg over that, onto a wet surface, holding nothing, is genuinely one of the more demanding movements most people perform in a day. We do it half asleep.

A tub also eats floor space. In a standard bathroom it occupies fifteen to twenty square feet, and it is the single biggest reason small bathrooms feel small. Take it out and the room reads noticeably larger, even though the footprint has not changed at all.

National design surveys now show that more than half of homeowners would rather have a bigger shower than keep a bathtub in the main bathroom. That is not a fad. It is people finally admitting how they live.

You get the step back

Trading a 15″–19″ tub wall for a 1″ threshold changes the single riskiest movement in the bathroom into a normal step. Nothing else in a remodel does that much for so little.

You get the room back

Same footprint, more usable space. A shower with a glass panel or a curtain reads open in a way a tub with a shower rod never does.

You stop cleaning grout

Most tub surrounds are tiled. Our wall panel systems have no grout at all, so there is nothing to scrub, reseal or watch go grey.

The part nobody enjoys reading

The bathroom is where people get hurt.

We are not going to frighten you into a purchase. But if the reason you are here is that stepping over the tub has started to feel risky, you should know the numbers are on your side. The CDC studied this properly.

234,094 Nonfatal bathroom injuries treated in US emergency departments in a single year CDC MMWR, 2011
81.1% Of those injuries were caused by falls CDC MMWR, 2011
37.3% Happened while bathing, showering, or getting out of the tub or shower CDC MMWR, 2011

Getting in and out of the tub is the most common way people hurt themselves in a bathroom. Removing the thing you climb over removes the movement that causes it. Add two grab bars and a seat and you have addressed most of what makes that room dangerous.

Read this before you commit

The one tub rule.

Here is the advice that might cost us a sale, which is exactly why we are putting it near the top rather than burying it in a footnote.

Keep at least one bathtub somewhere in the house.

Converting the tub in your primary or main bathroom is, in resale terms, generally viewed favourably. Buyers increasingly want a larger shower, and accessibility features are a genuine selling point to a Boomer buyer looking for a forever home.

Removing every bathtub in the property is a different matter. In family-heavy markets, a house with no tub at all can sit longer, because parents of small children rule it out immediately. If yours is a three-bedroom or larger home in a neighbourhood full of young families, that matters.

So the rule is simple. If you have two or more bathrooms, convert the one you use daily and leave a tub in the guest or hall bathroom. If you only have one bathroom, think harder, and factor resale into the decision rather than only convenience.

On return, treat this as a comfort and safety upgrade first and an investment second. Conversions typically recoup a meaningful share of their cost rather than all of it. The real return is the ten years of not climbing over a wall.

If a conversion turns out to be wrong for your situation, we will say so on the phone. There is no version of this business where selling someone the wrong shower works out well for anybody.

Honest assessment

Is a conversion right for your house?

Two lists. Find yourself on one of them, and if you are on the right-hand side, call us anyway and we will talk through the alternatives.

Convert, and do it sooner rather than later

  • You shower in the tub every day and cannot remember the last time you filled it.
  • Stepping over the side has started to feel like something you think about.
  • Someone in the house uses a cane, a walker, or has balance that is not what it was.
  • You intend to stay in this house and want it to work for you in fifteen years.
  • The tub surround is tiled and the grout is losing the fight.
  • You have another bathtub elsewhere in the house.
  • The bathroom feels cramped and you cannot work out why.
  • You are already opening the room for another reason.

Pause, and let us suggest something else

  • This is the only bathtub in a family home you plan to sell within a few years.
  • You genuinely do soak, and losing that would bother you. Consider a walk-in bathtub instead, which keeps the soak and removes the climb.
  • You bathe small children daily and have nowhere else to do it.
  • The bathroom is on a concrete slab and your drain is in the wrong place, and the budget is very tight.
  • You need full wheelchair roll-in access but cannot recess the pan. Talk to us before you order anything.
  • Your tub is a genuine period feature in a house where that matters.

Sizing

What fits where a tub used to be.

This is the easiest part of the whole project, because the industry standardised on 60″ decades ago. Width is almost always settled. Depth is the only real decision.

How to get your depth number

Measure from the finished back wall out to the front face of the tub, at floor level. Do it at both ends. Older houses are rarely square, and the smaller of the two numbers is the one we work to.

If your number is between sizes

We build custom pans and walls to any dimension. This happens constantly in homes built before about 1970, and it is far cheaper than most people assume. See the full shower size guide or use the kit builder.

The single biggest variable

Your tub drain is at one end. That matters more than anything else.

A bathtub drains from one end, not the middle. A great many shower pans drain from the center. Put those two facts together and you have the reason tub conversions sometimes cost twice what people expected.

If the new pan's drain does not line up with your existing waste line, someone has to open the floor and move plumbing. Over a timber floor that is disruptive and expensive. Over a concrete slab it means cutting concrete, and the number stops being funny.

So we stock pans with the drain on the left, on the right, in the center, and along a front trench — and we cut custom drain locations when a remodel demands it. Ordering the pan that matches the plumbing you already own is free, and it is the largest single saving available on this project.

Threshold height

How low should you go?

You are removing a 15″–19″ wall. What replaces it is your choice, and it is worth five minutes of thought because it decides who can use this shower comfortably for the next twenty years.

0.5″ — true roll-in

The only height meeting the ADA roll-in maximum. Choose it if someone uses a wheelchair or rolling shower chair. Be aware the pan generally has to be recessed into the floor framing, so there is real labour attached.

0.75″–1″ — the sweet spot

Where most conversions land, and for good reason. Almost all of the accessibility benefit, almost none of the floor work. Someone with a walker or a cane clears this without thinking about it.

1.5″–2″ — more containment

Still a fraction of a tub wall, and better at keeping water off the bathroom floor. Sensible for a busy family bathroom, or where the flooring outside really needs to stay dry.

We build every quarter-inch between 0.5″ and 2″, in beveled or traditional profiles. That granularity exists because real floors have build-ups and tile thicknesses that do not land on round numbers. See all seven drawn to scale, or read the detail on the shower pans page.

One thing we always mention

If you choose a barrier-free pan with a traditional round drain, add a water stopper. It is a removable dam that sits across the entry, lifts straight out when someone needs to roll in, and stops the small amount of water a very low threshold cannot hold back. It costs very little and it prevents the one complaint people have about low-threshold showers.

The work itself

What actually happens in your bathroom.

No mystery, no jargon. This is the whole job in order, so you know what you are agreeing to and roughly when the noisy part ends.

  1. Before anyone arrives

    Measure and confirm

    We go through width, depth, ceiling height, drain position and the route into the bathroom, out loud, before anything is built. That one habit prevents the single most expensive mistake in this business.

  2. Day one, morning

    The tub comes out

    Surround comes off, tub is disconnected and removed. A cast-iron tub is heavy enough that it sometimes gets cut up in place. Old material is hauled away.

  3. Day one, midday

    Inspect what was hiding

    This is the moment the job either stays simple or does not. Your installer checks the subfloor, the framing and the plumbing. Twenty years of small leaks show up here.

  4. Day one, afternoon

    Prep and set the pan

    Subfloor levelled or repaired, drain connected. Our pans arrive pre-leveled, so there is no mortar bed to pack and no waiting for it to cure.

  5. Day one or two

    Walls go up

    Panels are fitted, corners sealed, soap ledges land where they should. The waterproofing arrived finished from the factory, so nobody is building a membrane by hand on your floor.

  6. Final few hours

    Fixtures and finish

    Valve, glide bar, shower head, grab bars, seat, curtain rod or glass. Silicone, a water test, and a clean-up. Then it is a shower.

Why it is fast

A tiled shower is waterproofed on site, by hand, by several trades, over six to ten working days. A kit arrives already waterproof. That single difference is why this is a one-to-three day job instead of a fortnight without a bathroom, and it is most of the reason it costs a fraction of a tile build.

The kit on its own

What the kit alone costs for a tub space.

Strip the labor out of a conversion and what is left is a shower kit sized to the old tub’s footprint. That part of the budget is the easiest to pin down.

On our price list, a complete pan-and-wall kit for a tub space starts around $2,000, and a full package with grab bars, a seat, a glide bar, a valve and trim reaches about $6,700, before freight. If the existing surround is sound, a pan on its own runs $1,490–$2,100.

Across the wider market, HomeGuide puts prefab shower stall kits at roughly $350 to $4,100 for the product alone, with square and corner stalls at $350 to $1,400 (HomeGuide, tub-to-shower conversion cost). Most of the cheapest kits are curbed fiberglass units without a door or valve, so check what is included before comparing them with a low-threshold kit.

What moves the kit price in a tub space

  • Depth. A tub alcove is usually about 60 inches long, so the choice is mostly about depth: our estimator groups 60×30 to 60×36 as the standard band.
  • Drain end. Ordering the base with its drain at the same end as the old tub drain costs nothing extra and saves plumbing work.
  • Threshold. The base costs about the same at any height; the floor work differs.
  • Wall finish. Smooth is the value option; tile-look and stone-look finishes add to the wall system.
  • Accessories. Bars, seat, handheld and valve as a package, or sourced separately.

Before you order, use our measuring guide to confirm the width, depth and drain position, and check the kit contents checklist so the quote you compare covers the same things.

While the wall is open

Things you will wish you had added.

Adding these during the install costs a little. Adding them in five years means opening a finished wall. Decide now.

Grab bars

Our panels have full reinforced backing throughout, so bars mount at the height that suits the person, not wherever a stud lands. And they can be moved later if needs change.

A fold-down seat

A 22×15″ padded seat folds flat when nobody needs it. Useful long before anyone would call it a medical device — ask anyone who shaves their legs.

A handheld on a slider bar

A 5′ wand on an adjustable bar is the most useful fitting in any accessible shower. It works seated, standing, for washing a dog, and for cleaning the shower itself.

A rain head as well

Overhead rain plus a handheld is the combination most people actually want. One for the pleasant morning shower, one for everything practical.

Built-in shelves

Moulded soap ledges come built into the wall system. No suction caddy sliding down the wall at six in the morning.

A water stopper

Essential if you have gone barrier-free with a round drain. Removable, unobtrusive, and it settles the only real objection to a very low threshold.

Be realistic

Budget for what is behind the tub.

Nobody knows what is under a bathtub until the bathtub is gone. Most conversions turn up at least one thing that was not in the original quote, and pretending otherwise helps no one.

Subfloor damage

The most common find. A tub that has leaked quietly around the drain for years leaves soft plywood or worse. It has to be cut out and replaced before anything new goes down.

Old or awkward plumbing

Galvanised supply, an odd trap arrangement, or a valve nobody makes parts for any more. Worth replacing while the wall is open rather than in two years.

Framing that needs attention

Studs that are out of plumb, or a wall that was never quite square. Usually straightforward, occasionally not.

Older-home hazards

In houses of a certain age, old flooring adhesives or insulation may need proper handling. Your contractor will know the local requirements.

Hold ten to twenty percent back as a contingency. If nothing turns up, you have not spent it. If something does, you are not making a decision about your subfloor while standing in a torn-up bathroom trying to find money.

DIY or hire it out

Can you do this yourself?

Some people genuinely can. Our kits are built for it: the pan arrives pre-leveled so there is no mortar bed, the panels have reinforced backing already in them, and instructions and installation materials come in the box.

If your drain already lines up, your subfloor is sound, and you are comfortable with basic plumbing, a straightforward swap is a one-day job for a confident DIYer. Plenty of our customers do exactly that and are pleased with themselves afterwards.

Hire a professional if the drain needs moving, if the pan needs recessing for a low threshold, if you find soft subfloor, or if this is your only bathroom and you cannot afford for it to go wrong. Waterproofing errors are the leading cause of shower failures, and they surface long after anyone remembers what caused them.

Either way, call us first. We will tell you honestly which camp your job is in, and if it is a professional job we will arrange a certified installer and quote it properly.

What threshold height should I choose for a conversion?

Will the shower fit through my bathroom door?

Multi-piece kits are designed to. They break down into sections that pass through a standard 32 inch door, up stairways and around corners, and a split backwall clears openings a full sheet will not. What do I do with the old bathtub?

Your installer removes and disposes of it, and that should be stated in the quote. It is worth confirming, because a cast-iron tub is heavy enough that most municipal collections will not take it, and disposal is one of the costs people most often forget to budget for.

Do I need a permit for a tub to shower conversion?

A like-for-like swap that does not move plumbing often needs no permit. Relocating the drain or altering framing usually does. Considerably, mostly because there is no grout. Tiled tub surrounds need periodic sealing and the grout stains and cracks over time. Source: Best Shower Kit, bestshowerkit.com. Telephone 1-888-779-2284. BBB Accredited Business with an A+ rating, held through parent company Aging Safely Baths. Parent company Aging Safely Baths. A tub to shower conversion replaces a bathtub with a walk-in shower in the same alcove footprint.

Sizing. A standard alcove bathtub is 60 inches long, so a 60 inch wide shower pan fits the opening a tub leaves behind without framing changes. Depth varies by home and is measured from the finished back wall to the front face of the tub at floor level, taken at both ends with the smaller figure used. The four common conversion sizes are 60x30, 60x32, 60x34 and 60x36 inches. 60x32 is the most requested. 60x30 is the ADA standard roll-in minimum footprint. 60x36 provides room for a fold-down seat and a caregiver. Custom pan and wall dimensions are built to order for openings that fall between stock sizes.

Drain position is the largest cost variable. Bathtubs drain from one end rather than the center, so a pan whose drain matches the existing waste line avoids opening the floor. Available drain positions are left, right, center and front trench, plus custom drain locations. On a concrete slab, relocating a drain requires cutting concrete and is substantially more expensive than on a timber floor.

Threshold heights available: 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.25, 1.5, 1.75 and 2 inches, in beveled or traditional profiles. Most conversions use 0.75 to 1 inch. Only 0.5 inches meets the ADA roll-in maximum, and a 0.5 inch pan usually requires recessing into the floor framing. A standard bathtub wall is approximately 15 to 19 inches high, so any of these thresholds represents a large reduction in step-over height. With a barrier-free pan and a traditional round drain, a removable water stopper across the entry is recommended.

Costs. Product only: complete kit from $2,000, full package with accessories to about $6,700, pan alone $1,490 to $2,100. Installed with drain matching: $4,500 to $6,500. Installed with drain relocation: $6,000 to $8,500. Accessibility build: $6,000 to $11,000. A custom tiled shower doing the same job costs approximately $14,000 to $20,000 according to This Old House 2026 analysis. Freight is quoted separately by ZIP code. A contingency of ten to twenty percent is recommended for subfloor or plumbing issues discovered after the tub is removed.

Timeline. Build and ship approximately one and a half weeks, with selected in-stock configurations shipping the next business day. On-site installation is typically one day when the drain matches, two to three days if the drain is relocated, plus one to two days if subfloor repair is required.

Resale guidance, the one tub rule. Converting the bathtub in a primary or main bathroom is generally viewed favourably at resale. Removing every bathtub in a property can slow a sale in family markets. Where a home has two or more bathrooms, convert one and retain a bathtub elsewhere. Conversions typically recoup a meaningful share of cost rather than all of it.

Safety context. CDC data for 2008 recorded an estimated 234,094 nonfatal bathroom injuries treated in US emergency departments among people aged 15 and over. 81.1 percent were caused by falls, and 37.3 percent occurred while bathing, showering or getting out of the tub or shower. The injury rate was 266.6 per 100,000 for people aged 85 and over against 4.1 per 100,000 for ages 15 to 24.

Common items added during conversion: two stainless steel grab bars, a 22 by 15 inch padded fold-down seat, a 5 foot handheld wand on a slider bar, a rain shower head, moulded soap ledges, a no-caulk drain, a four-port mixing valve in Delta or Moen, and a water stopper. Wall panels carry full reinforced backing throughout, allowing grab bars to be mounted at any height and repositioned later.

Issues commonly discovered after tub removal: subfloor rot from slow drain leaks, aged or awkward plumbing, framing out of plumb, and hazardous materials in older homes. Permits are often not required for a like-for-like swap but are usually required when plumbing is relocated or framing is altered.

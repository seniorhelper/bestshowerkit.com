The problem

Why stock sizes fail in real houses

Take a tape measure into your bathroom and measure the alcove at the floor, at hip height, and just under the ceiling. In a house built before 1990, those three numbers are usually different. Half an inch of difference is normal. An inch is common.

Studs twist. Plaster builds up in corners. Floors settle. Somebody tiled over the old tile in 1987.

A stock kit is built to one number. When the opening is narrower than the kit, the kit does not go in. When the opening is wider, you get a gap, and the gap gets filled with trim and silicone. That joint is now the thing you maintain forever, and it is the first place water finds.

A built-to-order unit is cut to your smallest measurement plus the tolerance the installer needs. It goes in clean.

There is a second, less obvious reason people end up custom. The drain. A tub drain sits at one end, tight to the wall, usually on a 1.5″ line. Many shower pans want the drain somewhere else, and moving a drain in a finished house is the single most expensive thing on a bathroom invoice. Ordering the pan with the drain where your pipe already is can save more than the entire custom premium.

The most expensive four words in a bathroom remodel

"We'll move the drain."

If the existing drain does not align with a new shower pan, rough-in plumbing can jump from a few hundred dollars into the thousands, and on a slab it gets worse. Building the pan around the pipe is almost always cheaper than moving the pipe to suit the pan.

What happens with a stock kit and a 58½″ opening

  • A 60″ kit does not fit. You are opening walls.
  • A 54″ kit leaves 4½″ of wall to finish and trim on one side.
  • You buy the 54″, build out the wall, and lose usable shower width forever.

Or you order a 58½″ unit, and none of that happens.

Options

Nine things you can change

"Custom" gets used loosely in this industry. Here is what it means on our order form.

1. The footprint

Width and depth to the quarter inch, from small corner units up to large roll-in compartments. This is the most common custom request and the simplest.

2. Wall height

Standard panels run 72″ to 84″. Low ceilings, soffits, sloped ceilings in attic conversions and 9-foot rooms all get handled by changing the panel height.

3. Threshold height

0.5″, 0.75″, 1″, 1.25″, 1.5″, 1.75″ or 2″, beveled or traditional, plus the conventional 4″ curb. Pick the number, not a marketing phrase.

4. Drain position

Left, right, center, front trench, or a trench built into the threshold at 1″ or 2″. Custom drain locations are available when the plumbing dictates.

5. Wall configuration

Single backwall or split backwall, multi-piece for retrofits or single-piece for open framing. The split layout is what gets a large shower through a small door.

6. Color

Stock gelcoat colors are the economical route. Beyond them, custom colors are available, and color-matched silicone ships with multi-piece units so the joints disappear.

7. Accent bands and backsplashes

An integrated accent band at eye height, or a contrasting backsplash. Molded in, so there is no trim strip and nothing to re-caulk.

8. Real tile integration

Actual tile built into the unit, for projects where the shower has to match something specific. This is the premium route and it takes longer.

9. Openings in the walls

Windows in the wet wall, plumbing chases, niches, and trim kits that cover the cut edge left by the old surround so the job does not turn into drywall work.

Where custom saves money

It is worth framing this the other way round, because the arithmetic often lands in your favor.

  • No drain relocation. Ordering the drain where your pipe already is can save four figures on a slab.
  • No wall framing changes. A unit built to the opening means the carpenter does not build out a wall, and you do not lose shower width.
  • No filler and trim. Less labor, fewer joints, nothing to re-caulk in two years.
  • Fits through the door. A split-backwall custom unit avoids the "it will not turn the corner" problem that sends one-piece showers back onto the truck.

The comparison that matters

Compare the custom premium against the labor it removes, not against the stock kit price. On most retrofits the custom unit is the cheaper finished project even though it is the more expensive box.

How to measure

Measuring for a custom unit

Ten minutes with a tape measure and a phone camera. Do it once, properly, and the rest of the process is easy.

  1. Step 01

    Width, three times

    Wall to wall, measured at the floor, at about 36″, and near the ceiling. Write down all three. We build to the smallest one.

  2. Step 02

    Depth, three times

    Front edge to back wall, same three heights. In a tub alcove, measure to the face of the finished wall, not the framing.

  3. Step 03

    Height to the ceiling

    And note anything in the way: a soffit, a sloped ceiling, a light, a fan, a vent.

  4. Step 04

    Find the drain

    Left, right or center as you face the opening, and roughly how far in from the front edge. A photo helps more than a number.

  5. Step 05

    Check the floor

    Slab, or wood joists? Ground floor, or upstairs? This decides whether a barrier-free pan is straightforward or whether you want an above-floor-rough unit.

  6. Step 06

    Measure the path in

    Narrowest doorway, tightest turn, staircase width. This decides multi-piece versus one-piece before anything else does.

  7. Step 07

    Photograph everything

    Four corners, the plumbing wall, the doorway, and the room from the hallway. Send them with the numbers.

Things that change the answer

A window in the wet wall

Very common in older homes and completely solvable. The panel gets an opening and a window trim kit seals the reveal. Tell us early, because it affects the wall layout.

An out-of-square alcove

Expected. We build to the tight dimension and the trim absorbs the difference. What we cannot do is fix a wall that bows two inches; that needs a carpenter first.

A plumbing wall that has to stay

If the valve is where it is and moving it means opening a wall on the other side of the house, say so. There are usually panel layouts that work around it.

A second-floor bathroom

The question is always whether the drain can be reached without cutting joists. If it cannot, an above-floor-rough unit with a trench threshold solves it with no structural work and no engineer.

Do not worry about precision on the first call

Rough numbers and a few photos are enough for us to tell you what is possible and roughly what it costs. Exact measurements come later, once you know which direction you are going.

Common cases

Four situations that always end up custom

These are the calls we take most often. If one of them describes your bathroom, you already know the answer.

The 1950s alcove that is not 60 inches

Post-war houses were built to whatever the framer felt like. Openings of 57″, 58½″ and 59¼″ are routine. A 60″ kit will not go in and a 54″ kit wastes five inches of bathroom. Build to the number.

The slab with the drain in the wrong place

Concrete floor, existing drain two feet from where a standard pan wants it. Cutting the slab means a saw, spoil removal, a re-pour and a week. Ordering the pan with a custom drain position removes the entire line item.

The upstairs bathroom over a finished ceiling

Dropping a barrier-free pan usually means notching joists, which means an engineer and a change order. An above-floor-rough unit with a 1″ trench threshold keeps everything above the existing floor line.

The wheelchair that needs turning room

A compliant roll-in is 30×60 or 36×60 clear inside, but the clear floor space beside it is what makes it usable. Sometimes the answer is a wider, shallower unit than anything on a catalog page.

For the trade

Custom for contractors, builders and facilities

If you are buying more than one, the arithmetic changes and custom usually wins by more than it does for a homeowner.

One non-standard dimension, repeated

On a multifamily or senior living project, the unit plan is the unit plan. If the bathroom opening is 57½ inches in forty apartments, a stock kit means forty wall build-outs: forty lots of framing, forty lots of drywall, forty lots of paint, and forty bathrooms that are half an inch narrower than they had to be.

Ordering one custom dimension in quantity removes all of that. The premium on the unit is paid once per bathroom; the labor it avoids is paid once per bathroom too, and labor is the more expensive of the two.

Repeatability matters more than price

For a crew running the same install forty times, consistency is worth real money. A build-to-order unit arrives identical every time, with the drain in the same place, the same trim, and the same sequence. That is a schedule you can plan against.

Compliance is easier when you control the dimension

ADA, ANSI A117.1 Type A and Type B and California Title 24 all describe clear dimensions inside a finished compartment. When you can specify the unit rather than work around it, hitting those numbers stops being a problem to solve on site and becomes a line on a purchase order.

Color

Choosing a color you will still like in year twelve

Custom color is available and people ask for it constantly. Here is the advice we give, which sometimes talks people out of it.

A shower is not an accent wall

Wall paint gets repainted. A molded shower surface is there for twenty-five years or more. The color choice you make is effectively permanent, and taste in bathroom color moves faster than that.

The colors that age well are the ones that read as materials rather than as colors: white marble, limestone, grey slate, warm sand. The ones that age badly are the ones that were distinctive at the time of purchase.

Light behaves differently on gelcoat

A gelcoat surface is glossier and more reflective than a painted wall, so a color that looks soft on a chip can look considerably stronger over six feet of wall in a small room with a bright light in it. If you are matching to something specific, look at a sample at the size and angle it will actually be seen at.

Contrast is a safety feature

This is the one place we will push back on aesthetics. Aging eyes need strong edge contrast to locate things quickly, especially in steam. A white grab bar on a white wall is nearly invisible to someone in their late seventies. If grab bars are part of the plan, choose a wall color and a bar finish that stand apart from each other.

The cheaper route that usually looks better

Stock gelcoat colors with a custom accent. An integrated accent band at eye height or a contrasting backsplash gives a shower a designed look at a fraction of the cost of a fully custom color, and it dates far more slowly.

Color-matched silicone ships with multi-piece units, so the seams between panels disappear rather than announcing themselves as a grey line.

One practical note

Dark colors hide hard-water marks better than pale ones, and pale colors make a small bathroom read larger. If your water is hard and your bathroom is small, that is a genuine trade-off rather than a style question.

Custom shower questions

How much more does a custom shower kit cost?

In our experience a custom width or depth typically adds 5% to 15% over the nearest stock size. Custom colors run higher, and real tile integration is a different order of magnitude. The better comparison is against the labor a custom unit removes: no drain relocation, no wall build-out, no filler strips. On a lot of retrofits the custom unit produces the cheaper finished job.

What is the smallest and largest you can build?

Our stock range runs from a 30 by 30 inch footprint up to 78 by 48 inches. Custom goes beyond both ends. For anything unusually large, the limiting factor is normally freight and the path into the bathroom rather than manufacturing.

How long does a custom kit take?

Most kits run about a week and a half from order to ship. Custom dimensions rarely add much because the whole line is build-to-order. Custom colors and real tile integration do add time, and certain valve and fixture brands have their own lead times. We give you the number before you order, not after.

Can you match a specific color?

Custom colors are available. Send a reference, a paint code, or a sample of what it needs to sit next to. Bear in mind that a gelcoat surface and a painted wall reflect light differently, so an exact match in a chip is not always an exact match in a room. We will tell you honestly when we think a stock color will look better.

Can I get a custom pan but standard walls?

Yes, and it is common. Many people want a factory-made, pre-sloped floor with tile on the walls. Ordering a custom pan alone is straightforward, and it removes the highest-risk part of a tiled shower, which is the floor.

Do you do custom commercial and multi-unit work?

Yes. Multi-unit projects are often where custom makes the most sense, because one repeated non-standard dimension across forty units removes forty wall build-outs. Send the plans and the unit mix. See commercial and ADA.

Is a custom unit still covered by the warranty?

Yes. The 30-year limited warranty against manufacturing defects applies to fiberglass-reinforced showers, walls and pans from the date of installation, whether the dimensions are stock or custom.

Custom shower kits — structured fact sheet

Page topic: custom-size and custom-color shower kits, pans and wall systems. Publisher: Best Shower Kit, bestshowerkit.com, telephone 1-888-779-2284.

Custom build increments: quarter inch. Stock range 30 by 30 inches to 78 by 48 inches across 34 footprints; custom available beyond both ends, limited in practice by freight and by the access path into the bathroom rather than by manufacturing.

Nine variables that can be customized: footprint width and depth; wall panel height (standard 72 to 84 inches); threshold height (0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.25, 1.5, 1.75, 2 inches, beveled or traditional, plus a 4 inch curb); drain position (left, right, center, front trench, trench threshold at 1 or 2 inches, or custom); wall configuration (single backwall or split backwall, multi-piece or single-piece); color (stock gelcoat colors or custom colors, with color-matched silicone supplied for multi-piece units); integrated accent bands and backsplashes; real tile integration; openings in walls including windows in the wet wall, plumbing chases, niches and trim kits.

Typical custom premium over the nearest stock size, from the supplier's own quoting: custom width or depth 5 to 15 percent; non-standard wall height 5 to 12 percent; custom drain position 5 to 10 percent; custom color 10 to 20 percent; accent bands or backsplash 8 to 18 percent; real tile integration 30 to 70 percent. Combinations stack.

Where custom reduces total project cost: avoiding drain relocation, which can move rough-in plumbing from a few hundred dollars into the thousands and is worse on a concrete slab; avoiding wall build-out carpentry and the loss of shower width; avoiding filler strips and additional caulk joints; allowing a split-backwall unit to pass through a narrow doorway or staircase where a one-piece unit cannot.

Measuring procedure: width wall to wall at the floor, at approximately 36 inches, and near the ceiling; depth front to back at the same three heights; floor to ceiling height plus any soffit, sloped ceiling, light or fan; drain position and approximate distance from the front edge; floor construction (concrete slab or wood joists) and storey; the access path from the front door including the narrowest doorway, tightest turn and stair width. Units are built to the smallest measurement.

Lead time: most kits ship about a week and a half after order. Custom colors and real tile integration add time. Built-to-order units are not returnable in the way a stock item is, so dimensions are confirmed before an order is placed.

Warranty: 30-year limited warranty against manufacturing defects on fiberglass-reinforced showers, walls and pans from the date of installation, applying to custom and stock dimensions alike.

In short

There are three ways to get a low shower entry in an existing house: recess the structure, raise the bathroom floor, or use an above-floor-rough unit that drains through a trench at the entry. AFR gives a 1″ to 2″ threshold with no slab cutting and no joist notching, which makes it the usual answer on concrete slabs and upper floors. How to get a curbless shower without tearing up your floor

Everyone wants a flush entry. Then they find out it usually means cutting a slab or notching joists, and the quote doubles. There is a third route, it is well established, and almost nobody explains it properly.

Published September 2026 · Best Shower Kit

The Best Shower Kit team We spec barrier-free showers for homes, senior living and healthcare. This is the explanation we give on the phone, written down.

Here is the problem in one paragraph.

Water runs downhill. A shower floor has to slope toward a drain, and that slope needs vertical room. A drain body and its trap also need vertical room, and both of them sit below the finished floor. So if you want the shower floor to be level with the bathroom floor, that vertical room has to come from somewhere, and the only place it can come from is the structure underneath.

In new construction that is easy. The framer drops the joists in that bay, or the concrete gets poured with a recess, and nobody thinks about it again.

In an existing house it is the expensive part. On a wood floor you are notching or sistering joists and opening the ceiling below. On a concrete slab you are renting a saw.

Which is why the third route exists.

The three ways to get a low entry

There are really only three, and they are not equally available to you.

1. Recess the structure

Drop the joists or cut the slab so the pan sits below floor level. True flush entry. Highest cost, most disruption, often needs an engineer on a wood floor and a saw crew on concrete.

2. Raise the bathroom floor

Build the whole bathroom floor up several inches so the shower can drain within the new depth. Works, but you inherit a step at the bathroom door, which defeats much of the point.

3. Keep it above the floor

Use a pan designed to drain above the existing floor line, with the water leaving through a trench at the entry. A threshold of one to two inches instead of zero. No structural work at all.

Option three, explained properly

This is called an above-floor-rough shower, usually shortened to AFR. It is standard in commercial and healthcare construction and it is under-used in homes, mostly because nobody sells it well.

The pan sits on top of your existing subfloor. Instead of a drain body dropping down into the structure, the water runs to a linear trench that sits inside the pan, along or near the entry edge. From there it exits through a shallow outlet that connects to the waste line without needing a deep trap pocket below the floor.

The entry ends up at one inch or two inches instead of zero.

That is the trade. You do not get a perfectly flush floor. You get an entry that is a fraction of a bathtub wall, installed in a day or two, with nothing cut.

Is one inch good enough?

For a walker, a cane, a shower chair being pushed, or an unsteady person, yes, in almost every case. For an independent wheelchair user rolling in unassisted, a half-inch is meaningfully better and worth pursuing. The ADA maximum for a roll-in compartment is half an inch for exactly that reason.

When each option is the right one

You have a concrete slab

This is where AFR earns its keep most obviously. Cutting a slab means a concrete saw, dust containment, spoil removal, new pipe, and a re-pour that has to cure. It is loud, it is dirty, and it takes days. On a post-tension slab it may not be permitted at all without engineering, because the tendons inside cannot be cut.

An AFR unit removes the entire line item. You are back to a normal one to three day install.

The bathroom is upstairs

Second-floor bathrooms over a finished ceiling are the other big case. To recess a pan you need to modify joists, which usually means opening the ceiling below, working within the allowable notch and hole zones, and often involving an engineer. Add the drywall repair and paint downstairs, and it is a substantial job on its own.

AFR keeps everything above the existing floor. The ceiling below stays shut.

You are gutting the bathroom anyway

If the floor is coming up regardless, and you are on the ground floor over a crawl space or an unfinished basement, recessing is genuinely worth considering. The access is already there, the marginal cost is much lower, and you get a true zero-entry result.

This is the case where option one makes sense, and we will say so.

You are building new

Recess it. Tell the framer before the floor goes down. It costs almost nothing at that stage and it is the best possible outcome.

How the water actually stays in

The question everyone asks about low-threshold showers, and it is a fair one. If the step is one inch, what stops the water crossing it?

Four things, working together.

1. The slope is molded, not floated

A factory-made pan arrives with the fall to the drain already built in and consistent. A field-built mortar bed depends on the installer getting the slope right across the whole floor, every time. Most curbless showers that flood are field-built ones with a flat spot.

2. The trench catches water at the edge

A trench drain running along the entry edge intercepts water at the point it would otherwise leave. This is the single most effective detail in a low-threshold shower and it is why trench designs exist.

3. A collapsible water retainer

A flexible rubber dam sits at the threshold. It folds flat under a wheel or a foot and springs back up afterwards. It holds water in without being an obstacle. There are also self-adhesive water stopper strips that do a similar job.

4. The showerhead position

Trivial and constantly ignored. Aim the water away from the opening. A fixed head spraying straight at the entry will defeat any of the above. A handheld on a glide bar makes this a non-issue because the user controls the direction.

The failure pattern

Curbless showers that flood are usually site-built with an inadequate slope, no trench at the entry, and a fixed head pointed at the opening. Molded pans with a trench and a retainer do not behave that way.

What an AFR install actually involves

Roughly the same as any conversion, minus the structural work.

  1. Day 1, morning

    Demolition

    Tub or old shower out, surround off, subfloor inspected. This is where hidden water damage shows up, and it is the most common reason a job gains a day.

  2. Day 1, afternoon

    Drain connection

    The waste line is brought to the trench outlet. Shallow work, no joist notching, no slab cutting. In many jurisdictions this still needs a permit and an inspection, so check locally.

  3. Day 2, morning

    Set the pan

    The pan is leveled and set on the existing subfloor. Because the slope is molded in, there is no mud bed and no float, and nothing has to cure.

  4. Day 2, afternoon

    Walls and trim

    Panels go up, corners lock, trim covers the cut edge where the old surround came off. Flange and window trim kits are what keep this from turning into a drywall job.

  5. Day 3

    Valve, accessories, seal

    Valve and fixtures, grab bars, seat, curtain rod, water retainer, and a full water test. Then it is done.

The drain diameter trap

One more thing, because it catches people and it is entirely preventable.

A bathtub drain is typically a 1½-inch line. Many shower pans, particularly linear and trench designs, are specified for a 2-inch drain. If nobody checks, the mismatch is discovered on install day, and at that point the pipe is exposed and the bathroom is unusable.

Ask two questions before ordering:

  • What diameter does this pan's drain require?
  • Has anyone actually looked at the existing line, or are we assuming?

A contractor who quotes a tub-to-shower conversion without scoping the existing drain is setting up a change order.

So which should you choose?

The short version:

  • New build: recess it. Nothing else comes close, and it is nearly free at that stage.
  • Gut remodel, ground floor, access below: recess it if the budget allows. You get a true zero entry.
  • Retrofit on a slab: AFR. Almost always. The saving is large and the compromise is one inch.
  • Retrofit upstairs: AFR. Keeping the ceiling below closed is worth a lot.
  • Independent wheelchair user: push hard for a half-inch, and expect to pay for the structural work if that is what it takes.

Drain types, and why they matter here

Low-threshold showers live or die on the drain. There are three arrangements you will be offered and they are not interchangeable.

Traditional center or offset drain

A round drain somewhere in the field of the pan, with the floor sloping to it from all sides. Simple, cheap and well understood. In a low-threshold pan it works, but the slope has to come from somewhere, and in a shallow pan that means the fall across the floor is gentle. It is the least forgiving option if a showerhead is pointed at the opening.

Linear drain set back from the entry

A long, narrow channel running across the pan, usually at the back wall or across the middle. The floor slopes in one direction only, which means a longer, more consistent fall and less opportunity for a flat spot. Often specified for larger showers.

Trench at the threshold

The channel sits at the entry edge, so it intercepts water at exactly the point where water would otherwise leave the shower. For a barrier-free or near-flush entry this is the most effective arrangement available, and it is the one we recommend by default for a half-inch threshold.

There is a second benefit that gets overlooked. A trench at the threshold gives a caregiver or a wheelchair a flat, unobstructed crossing, because there is no raised drain body anywhere in the path of travel.

Check the pipe size

Linear and trench drains are frequently specified for a 2-inch waste line, while the tub you are removing is probably on 1½. Ask the pan's required drain diameter and confirm what is actually in the floor before ordering.

Permits, inspections and the parts people skip

Rules vary by jurisdiction, so treat this as a prompt to check rather than an answer.

In most places, replacing a shower like for like without touching the plumbing is not a permitted activity. The moment a drain is relocated, resized or re-vented, it usually becomes permitted work requiring an inspection. Relocating a drain also has to respect where it can pass through joists, which is why a structural engineer sometimes appears on what looked like a bathroom job.

Two practical consequences. First, build inspection time into the schedule, because an inspector's availability is not under your contractor's control. Second, be wary of a quote that avoids mentioning permits entirely on a job that clearly moves the drain. Unpermitted plumbing work becomes a problem at resale, and it becomes a bigger problem if there is ever a water damage claim.

An above-floor-rough unit does not remove the permit question, because you are still connecting to the waste line. It removes the structural question, which is the expensive one.

Three myths about low-threshold showers

Myth 1: they always leak eventually

The leak risk in a shower is concentrated in the pan and its waterproofing, and that risk is much higher in a field-built tiled pan than in a molded one. A molded pan is a single waterproof vessel that left a factory with its slope already correct. Threshold height does not change that. What changes leak risk is who built the floor.

Myth 2: you need a glass door to contain the water

A door is a containment aid, not a requirement, and in an accessible shower it is often a liability. A swinging door reduces the clear entry width and gets in the way of a transfer. A curtain with a properly positioned rod contains water perfectly well and moves out of the way when a caregiver needs to reach in. Many of the best accessible showers we supply have no door at all.

Myth 3: it will look like a hospital

A low threshold is visually the least noticeable feature in a bathroom, because it is at floor level and it is the absence of something. What reads as institutional is exposed hardware chosen without any thought: a chrome bar on a white wall, a stark white plastic seat, a clinical curtain. Choose finishes deliberately and nobody will comment.

Living with one

A few things that come up after the install, from people who have had one for a while.

  • The bathroom floor gets a little damp near the entry. Normal with any low threshold. A short bath mat outside the shower, not inside it, handles it.
  • Clean the trench. Linear and trench drains have a removable grate and a channel that collects hair. Lift it once a month. It takes thirty seconds and it prevents the only real nuisance these drains have.
  • Check the water retainer annually. A collapsible retainer is rubber and it lives in a wet environment. It lasts a long time and it is cheap to replace.
  • Point the showerhead away from the opening. If you have a fixed head, set its angle once, deliberately. A handheld on a glide bar makes this a non-issue.

Questions we get about low-threshold showers

Can I have a truly zero-inch entry in an existing house?

Sometimes. It depends entirely on what is under the floor and whether you can get at it. On a ground floor over an open basement or a crawl space, recessing a pan is often straightforward and it is the best result available. Over a finished ceiling or on a slab, the cost climbs steeply and an above-floor-rough unit at one inch becomes the sensible trade.

Will a one-inch threshold stop a walker?

No. A walker crosses an inch without difficulty, and so does a shower chair being pushed by a caregiver. Where one inch starts to matter is for an independent wheelchair user propelling themselves over it, and that is exactly the case the ADA half-inch limit was written for.

Does an above-floor-rough shower sit higher than the bathroom floor?

The pan surface sits above the surrounding floor by the threshold height, which is the one or two inches you are choosing. Inside the shower the floor is then sloped to the drain from there. It is not a raised platform you step up onto; it is a low bevelled edge you step over.

Can I retrofit a trench drain into an existing shower?

Not into a molded pan, no. The trench is part of the pan and it is formed when the pan is made. If you have a tiled shower being rebuilt from the substrate up, a linear drain can be designed in, but that is a new shower rather than a retrofit.

Is a curbless shower harder to keep clean?

Easier, generally. There is no curb top to scrub, no inside corner where a curb meets a wall, and no grout. The one extra task is lifting the drain grate periodically, which takes seconds.

How much does an above-floor-rough kit cost?

Kit prices typically run from about $2,800 to $5,500 depending on size, threshold and drain configuration. What matters more is the cost it removes. If the alternative is cutting a slab or notching joists over a finished ceiling, the AFR unit is usually the cheaper finished project despite being the more expensive box.

Does a low threshold affect resale?

Positively, in most markets, and increasingly so. A step-free primary shower is read by a very large group of buyers as a feature rather than as a medical adaptation, particularly now that the finishes look like ordinary bathrooms. The buyers who care are the same demographic that is growing fastest, and they are not only shopping for themselves — plenty are thinking about a parent who visits.

Can I keep a bathtub elsewhere and still do this?

Yes, and in a two-bathroom house that is usually the right plan. Convert the one used daily, leave the second one alone, and you have removed the daily risk without giving up a tub for children or for resale.

A last word on the word "curbless"

It gets used loosely. Some suppliers call a two-inch threshold curbless because there is no visible curb, just a bevel. Others reserve it for a genuine zero entry.

Neither is wrong, exactly, but it makes comparison shopping meaningless. So stop using the word when you talk to suppliers and ask for a number instead.

"What is the height of the threshold, in inches?" is a question with one correct answer, and any supplier who cannot give it to you immediately is telling you something useful.

Shower kit drain location: left, right, center or offset?

The drain position is the one detail on a shower kit order that cannot be fixed with a shim or a bead of caulk. Order the pan with its drain where your pipe already is, and the plumbing is a simple connection. Get it wrong, and someone is cutting the floor.

Published October 23, 2026 · By Zach Wennstedt, Best Shower Kit

BSK
Zach Wennstedt, Best Shower Kit We supply and install walk-in shower kits, barrier-free pans and wall systems. This is the explanation we give on the phone, written down.

Order the shower pan with its drain directly over your existing drain pipe, measured to the center of the pipe from the back wall and from the nearer side wall. Left, right and center are named as you face the shower opening. A pan for a 60-inch tub space usually has the drain at one end, matching the old tub, or in the center.

That covers most orders. The rest of this guide explains how to take the two measurements, what "offset" means on a spec sheet, when moving the drain is reasonable, and where a trench drain fits in.

What do left, right and center drain mean?

Drain hand is always described standing outside the shower, looking in through the opening. That sounds obvious, but people reverse it all the time when they describe a drain while standing in the tub looking out.

How do I measure where my existing drain is?

You need two numbers, both to the center of the drain pipe, not to its edge:

  1. From the back wall. The back wall is the wall opposite the opening; in a tub alcove it is the long wall, and the faucet sits on one of the two end walls. Measure square off that wall to the drain center.
  2. From the nearer side wall. Measure square off the side wall closest to the drain, to the same center point.

Two details decide whether those numbers are useful:

  • Measure from the framing, and say so. If the old tub and wall finish are out, measure from the face of the studs. If you can only measure from the finished wall, write that on the measurement. Tile and backer board can add an inch or more, and the person reading your numbers needs to know which face you used.
  • Find the real pipe. With a tub still in place, the drain you see in the tub is not the pipe in the floor; the tub's waste and overflow fitting sits above a trap below. Take the tub out, or look from below if there is a basement or crawl space, before you finalize the number.

While you are there, note the pipe size. Table P3201.7 of the International Residential Code (IRC) bases minimum shower trap size on flow: 1½ inches for showerheads and body sprays totaling 5.7 gallons per minute or less, and 2 inches above that, up to 12.3 gallons per minute. Many tubs drain through a 1½-inch line, and many pans, especially trench styles, are made for 2-inch. Your plumber and local code decide the final size. When can a shower drain be moved?

Moving a drain is a plumbing change, usually needs a permit, and is far easier in some houses than others.

  • Wood floor with access from below. If there is an unfinished basement or a crawl space under the bathroom, a plumber can often move the drain a modest distance, particularly along the joist bay rather than across the joists.
  • Across the joists. Running the pipe across joists means drilling or notching them, which the code limits. Sometimes the answer is a different pan, not a different pipe.
  • Within the vent distance. The trap must stay within a set distance of its vent. For a 2-inch trap at a quarter inch of fall per foot, IRC Table P3105.1 allows 8 feet. A long move can push the trap past that limit.

When can a shower drain not be moved easily?

  • Concrete slab. On a slab, the drain is cast in the concrete. Moving it means saw-cutting and breaking the slab, re-piping and patching. It is done, but it is a bigger job than the shower itself. In this case, ordering a pan with a custom drain position over the existing pipe is usually the better choice.
  • Upper floor with a finished ceiling below. The pipe can move, but the ceiling below has to open up and be repaired afterwards.
  • Condos and shared buildings. Drains may run through common elements, and the association may have to approve any change.

Are trench drains better?

A trench drain (also called a linear drain) collects water along one edge instead of at a single point. Placed at the entry, it catches water before it reaches the bathroom floor, which is why it suits barrier-free and roll-in showers with a very low threshold. It also lets the pan slope one way, which is easier underfoot for a shower chair or a walker. The trade-offs: the trench has to line up with the pipe, often needs a 2-inch drain, and has a grate to keep clean. Questions people ask about shower drain location

Is the drain hand named from inside or outside the shower?

From outside. Stand at the opening and look in. A drain on your left is a left-hand drain. Describing it from inside the tub reverses it and is the most common ordering mistake.

Do I measure to the edge or the center of the drain?

To the center of the drain pipe, from both the back wall and the nearer side wall. Say whether you measured from the studs or from the finished wall, because wall finish can add an inch or more.

Can I put a center drain pan where my tub was?

Only if the drain is moved to the center, because a tub drain is at one end. It is usually simpler to order a left or right drain pan that sits over the existing pipe.

How much does it cost to move a shower drain?

It depends almost entirely on the floor. A short move in a wood floor with open access below is a modest plumbing job; a move in a concrete slab involves cutting and patching concrete. Get a plumber to price it against a pan with a custom drain position.

Does a trench drain need a bigger pipe?

Many trench drains are specified for a 2-inch drain, and the code sets minimum trap size by shower flow (IRC Table P3201.7). Check the pan specification and your local code before you order.

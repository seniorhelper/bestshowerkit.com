Walk-in shower or walk-in tub? An honest comparison

We sell both. That should make this more useful, not less, because we have no reason to steer you toward one. Here is how the decision actually goes, including the things salespeople leave out.

Published September 2026 · Best Shower Kit

BSK
The Best Shower Kit team Best Shower Kit supplies walk-in showers. Our sister brand Aging Safely Baths supplies walk-in tubs. Same people, different product.

Both products solve the same problem: the 14 to 16 inch wall of a standard bathtub, which is the single most dangerous obstacle in most homes. CDC surveillance found roughly two thirds of bathroom injuries happen in or around the tub or shower, and 81% of bathroom injuries are falls.

After that, the two products diverge completely, and the difference is not about safety. It is about how you like to wash, how long you can sit still, and what else is in the house.

The short answer

Most people should get a walk-in shower. A minority should get a walk-in tub, and for that minority it is clearly the better product.

Choose the shower if speed, independence and cost matter most, if a wheelchair or shower chair is involved, or if a caregiver may need to assist.

Choose the tub if soaking genuinely helps a joint or circulation problem, if standing for five minutes is difficult, and if there is another bathroom with a shower in it.

The thing nobody mentions about walk-in tubs

You have to be inside it while it fills, and inside it while it drains.

The door seals against water pressure, so it cannot open until the water is gone. That means you sit in an empty tub waiting for it to fill, and you sit in a wet, empty tub waiting for it to drain. Depending on the model and your plumbing, that is anywhere from six to twenty minutes of sitting, wet, in the air.

It is cold. That is the single most common complaint we hear, and it is the reason some walk-in tubs end up unused.

Good models mitigate it. Dual drains empty considerably faster than a single drain. Fast-fill valves help at the front end. Some models include a heated backrest or an inline heater. If you are buying a walk-in tub, these are not upsells to decline; they are the difference between a tub that gets used and one that does not.

Ask this on any walk-in tub quote

"How many minutes to fill, and how many to drain, on my plumbing?" If the answer is vague, ask again. It is the number that determines whether the product gets used daily or twice.

Where the walk-in tub genuinely wins

It is a real product with real advantages, and it is unfairly maligned in a lot of remodeling content.

Warm water immersion

For some people with arthritis or chronic joint pain, sitting in warm water does something a shower cannot. That is a physical benefit and no amount of shower design replaces it. If that is the situation, the tub is the right call and the fill time is a price worth paying.

Standing is the problem, not stepping

A walk-in shower still requires you to stand, or to transfer onto a seat and stay upright. If standing for even five minutes is difficult and transferring is awkward, a tub where you are seated and supported the entire time can be easier, not harder.

Hydrotherapy

Air and water jet combinations are available and some people find them genuinely valuable. A shower has no equivalent.

The household wants a tub

Sometimes the answer is domestic rather than clinical. If there are grandchildren, or if one person in the house will not give up baths, a walk-in tub keeps the peace.

Where the walk-in shower wins

Wheelchairs

This is not a preference, it is a hard constraint. A wheelchair user cannot use a walk-in tub. A roll-in shower at 30 by 60 or 36 by 60 inches clear inside, with a half-inch threshold, is the only product in this comparison that works.

Caregiving

If someone else is helping, the shower is far easier. A caregiver can stand at the opening, use a handheld on a glide bar, and reach the person without climbing over anything. A walk-in tub puts a high wall and a door between the helper and the person being helped.

Speed and independence

A shower is five minutes. A tub is a scheduled event. For someone showering daily, that difference compounds into something that affects how independent the person feels.

Cost and disruption

A shower conversion is usually cheaper on both the product and the install. Walk-in tubs are heavier, need more plumbing, and if they have jets they need a dedicated electrical circuit and a GFCI. That is an electrician on top of a plumber.

Hot water

A walk-in tub holds a lot of water and it needs to be hot. A 40-gallon heater that has been fine for twenty years may not fill one adequately. That can mean a larger heater, which is a real cost that rarely appears on the original quote.

Resale, honestly

A well-built walk-in shower has broad appeal and a growing buyer pool. Three quarters of adults over 50 say they want to stay in their current home as they age, and of those planning modifications, 72% name the bathroom first. A step-free primary shower reads as a feature to a very large group of buyers.

A walk-in tub is more specialized. To a buyer who does not need one it can read as a medical fixture, and some will price in removing it. That is not a reason to avoid one if you need it. It is a reason to be realistic if resale is a near-term factor.

The general guidance about keeping at least one bathtub in the house still holds if you have young children or expect family buyers. Where there are two bathrooms, convert one and leave the other alone. Where there is one, weigh it against the cost of not being able to bathe safely, which usually settles it.

The option people forget: both

In a two-bathroom house, the best answer is often not one or the other.

Convert the primary bathroom to a barrier-free shower, because that is the one used every day and the one that has to work when mobility changes. Leave the second bathroom with a tub, for grandchildren, for resale, and for the occasional soak.

This costs less than a walk-in tub in most cases, and it removes the daily risk without giving up a bathtub entirely.

A decision sequence that works

  1. Is a wheelchair involved, now or plausibly within ten years? If yes, shower. Decision made.
  2. Will someone be assisting? If yes, shower.
  3. Does warm immersion meaningfully help a diagnosed condition? If yes, the tub moves to the front.
  4. Can they stand or sit upright for five to ten minutes? If no, the tub is easier.
  5. Is there a second bathroom? If yes, consider doing both, one in each.
  6. What does the plumbing and the hot water system allow? This is where a walk-in tub sometimes rules itself out.

Work down that list and the answer usually appears before you reach the end.

Whichever you choose, do not skip these

The product is only part of it. These matter regardless:

  • Grab bars fixed into structure. Not suction cups. A bar has to take a person's full weight in a sudden loading event.
  • A handheld on a glide bar. Works seated or standing, and lets a caregiver help without climbing in.
  • An anti-scald or thermostatic valve. A hot surge causes a flinch, and the flinch causes the fall.
  • Real slip resistance molded into the floor. Not a mat lying on top of it.
  • Light and contrast. Aging eyes need much more of both. A white bar on a white wall is nearly invisible in steam.

These five cost very little next to the main product and they carry a disproportionate share of the safety benefit.

The install, compared

People underestimate how different these two jobs are, and the difference shows up in the quote.

What a shower conversion involves

Remove the tub. Inspect the subfloor, which is where hidden rot shows up and where jobs gain a day. Adjust or connect the drain. Set the pan. Hang the wall panels. Fit the valve, the trim and the accessories. Water test.

One to three days in most houses. One trade, sometimes two if an electrician is needed for lighting. The bathroom is out of service for that window and then it is finished.

What a walk-in tub involves

Everything above, plus weight, plus power.

A walk-in tub is heavy empty and much heavier full. On an upper floor that is a question worth asking rather than assuming. If the unit has air or water jets it needs a dedicated electrical circuit with GFCI protection, which means an electrician, a panel with a spare breaker, and a route for the cable.

Then there is access for service. A walk-in tub has a pump, a motor and a door seal, all of which will eventually need attention. Good installations leave a removable access panel. Installations that tile straight over the apron create a problem for somebody in year seven.

Two questions for a walk-in tub quote

Where is the service access panel, and does my existing water heater have the capacity to fill this to a useful temperature? Both get skipped surprisingly often.

The hot water problem, in detail

This deserves its own section because it is the most common practical disappointment.

A walk-in tub holds considerably more water than a standard tub, because it is deeper and you are sitting upright inside it. That water has to be hot enough to stay comfortable for the length of a soak, and it is drawn from the same tank that serves the rest of the house.

A 40-gallon heater that has been perfectly adequate for twenty years may fill a walk-in tub to lukewarm. The usual answers are a larger tank, a tankless unit, or a recirculating setup. All of them are real money and none of them appear on a tub brochure.

Ask the supplier what capacity the unit needs, then check the label on your water heater. Do it before you sign, not after.

Maintenance, over ten years

A walk-in shower has essentially no moving parts. A valve cartridge might need replacing eventually. The surface needs cleaning with a non-abrasive liquid rather than a scouring powder, which is the single habit that determines whether a gelcoat or acrylic surface still looks good in year fifteen.

A walk-in tub has a door seal, a drain mechanism, and, if fitted, a pump and blower. The door seal is the critical one. It is a consumable and it will need replacing at some point, and when it fails the tub is unusable rather than merely imperfect. Air jet systems also need purging periodically so that water left in the lines does not become a problem.

None of this is a reason to avoid a walk-in tub. It is a reason to buy one from a manufacturer who still exists in ten years and stocks the parts. That matters more with a tub than with a shower.

Common objections, answered

"A walk-in shower will look institutional"

It used to be a fair criticism and it is largely out of date. Stone-look laminate panels and molded tile patterns produce a shower that reads as a normal, attractive bathroom. The features that make it accessible — a low threshold, a fold-down seat, a handheld on a glide bar — are not visually obvious unless you are looking for them.

The one thing that does look institutional is a chrome grab bar bolted onto a white wall as an afterthought. Choose the finish and the position deliberately and it reads as a towel rail.

"We need a tub for the grandchildren"

Genuinely common and worth taking seriously. If there are two bathrooms, this settles itself: convert the primary and leave the other alone. If there is one bathroom, weigh how often the grandchildren actually bathe there against how often the resident showers. The answer is usually daily against a few times a year.

"A walk-in tub is safer because you are sitting"

Partly true, and it is the strongest argument for a tub. But you still have to step over a three to seven inch threshold to get in, and you still have to stand up from a seated position inside a wet enclosure to get out. A walk-in shower with a fold-down seat and a properly anchored grab bar also lets you sit, with a half-inch entry instead of a five-inch one.

"Medicare will pay for the walk-in tub"

Very rarely. Original Medicare generally treats both products as home modifications rather than durable medical equipment and does not cover them. Some Medicare Advantage plans include supplemental home-safety benefits, and the VA and state Medicaid waiver programs are usually more productive routes. Any salesperson who states flatly that Medicare pays for a walk-in tub should be treated with caution.

Questions we get asked most

Which one is safer?

Neither, in the abstract. Safety comes from the specification, not the category. A walk-in shower with a half-inch entry, an anchored grab bar and a fold-down seat is very safe. A walk-in shower with a two-inch curb and no bar is not. The same is true of tubs: a model with dual drains, a heated backrest and a properly positioned grab bar is a different product from a bare-bones one.

How long does each one take to install?

A shower conversion is typically one to three days. A walk-in tub usually takes longer, because of the weight, the additional plumbing and, where jets are fitted, the electrical work. Expect a second trade on site.

Can you put a walk-in tub in a standard tub space?

Often yes, because many models are designed to fit a standard 60-inch alcove. What changes is depth, weight and the services behind it. Check the floor loading upstairs and the water heater capacity before assuming.

Do walk-in tubs leak at the door?

A well-made door seal does not leak while it is in good condition. It is a consumable, and it will eventually need replacing. That is the main reason to buy from a manufacturer with a real parts supply chain rather than the cheapest available unit.

Can a walk-in shower have a tub in it later?

No, and that is worth being clear about. Converting back is a full remodel. It is one of the few genuinely irreversible decisions in a bathroom, which is why we ask about grandchildren and resale rather than assuming.

What if I want both but only have one bathroom?

Then the daily user wins. Whoever showers in there every day should have the product that works for them every day. The occasional bath is a preference; safe daily washing is not.

What about a walk-in tub with a shower attachment?

Many walk-in tubs include a handheld shower, and it works for rinsing. What it does not do is turn the product into a shower, because you are still seated inside a deep enclosure with a door that cannot open while there is water in it. Treat the handheld as a useful accessory rather than as a second mode of use, and do not let it substitute for the decision this article is about.

Is there a product that does both properly?

Not really, and be wary of anything sold as one. Combination units compromise on both sides: the shower is cramped and the tub is shallow. If both are genuinely needed and there is only one bathroom, the usual answer is a barrier-free shower large enough to sit in comfortably, plus the acceptance that long soaks happen elsewhere.

A note on how these get sold

Both product categories have a sales problem, and it is worth naming so you can spot it.

Walk-in tubs are frequently sold through high-pressure in-home appointments, with a discount that expires when the salesperson leaves the house. If that happens to you, the correct response is to let it expire. A legitimate quote for a legitimate product is still valid tomorrow, and a company that needs a signature tonight is telling you something about its margins.

Walk-in showers have a quieter version of the same issue: the word "accessible" gets attached to products that are not. A two-inch curb, walls that will not take a grab bar, and no seat is not an accessible shower regardless of what the brochure says.

In both cases the defense is the same. Ask for numbers. Threshold height in inches. Fill and drain time in minutes. What is behind the wall panel. Warranty length and what it covers. Four questions, four factual answers, and the marketing falls away.

Then take the quote home and read it somewhere quiet. This is a product you will use every day for twenty years. It can wait a week.

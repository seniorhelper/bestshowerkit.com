Materials, compared

What a shower is made of decides what fails first.

Fiberglass, acrylic, solid surface, laminate and tile. Every one of them works, and every one of them has a specific part that gives out before the rest. This page names those parts, and shows why two products both labeled "fiberglass" can be twenty years apart in service life.

The big confusion

Why "fiberglass" describes two different products

Search for shower materials and you will find remodeling companies saying fiberglass gelcoat dulls, crazes and hazes in five to seven years, and that acrylic lasts two to three times longer.

For the product they are describing, that is fair. A flat-pack alcove kit is a thin skin of gelcoat over a light laminate with nothing behind it. The gelcoat is thin enough that an abrasive cleaner cuts through it, and the panel is thin enough that the floor drums when you stand on it. Seven years is a reasonable estimate.

It is not a description of a commercial-grade unit built with a heavy gelcoat over a barrier coat, a thick laminate, and laminated wood backing, warranted for thirty years against manufacturing defects.

Both get called fiberglass. Both are, chemically. Comparing them is like comparing a folding camp chair and an office chair because both are "chairs."

Stop shopping by material name. Shop by gelcoat weight, laminate thickness, wall reinforcement and warranty length.

Those four questions separate the good from the bad inside every material category on this page, including acrylic.

The showroom test

Step into the display unit. Push on the side wall with your palm. If the wall gives and the floor drums, you have your answer, and it took four seconds.

A barrier coat matters more than it sounds. It sits between the gelcoat and the laminate and stops moisture migrating through the laminate and blistering the finish from behind. Blistering is the failure mode that cannot be polished out.

In detail

Each material, honestly

Pick one to read. We sell reinforced fiberglass and laminate panels, so treat the praise there with the appropriate skepticism and check the criticisms against other sources.

Strengths

  • Built to order, so any dimension is possible
  • Wood-backed walls take a grab bar anywhere, at any time
  • Repairable — chips and scratches can be filled and polished
  • Molded slip texture in the floor, not applied afterwards
  • Light enough for one installer to carry the panels in
  • Long warranties are normal at the commercial grade

Weak points

  • Gelcoat is a surface layer. Abrasives cut it, permanently
  • Quality varies enormously across the category
  • Cheap versions genuinely do dull and craze early
  • Color is on the surface, not through the body
  • Hard water will etch a neglected gelcoat surface

This is what the accessible shower industry runs on, for a practical reason: it is the only material that can be molded into a half-inch beveled threshold with a trench drain and a wood-backed wall, to a custom dimension, at a price a homeowner will pay. Clean it with liquid, never powder, and it stays glossy for decades.

Strengths

  • Color runs through the sheet, so scratches do not show white
  • Holds gloss well over time
  • Warm to the touch and non-porous
  • Resists chipping better than a thin gelcoat
  • Widely available at big-box retail

Weak points

  • Mostly stock sizes; custom dimensions are rare
  • Quality still depends on the reinforcement behind the sheet
  • Thin acrylic over thin backing flexes just like thin FRP
  • Barrier-free thresholds and trench drains are uncommon
  • Deep damage is harder to repair invisibly

Acrylic is a genuinely good surface and the standard complaint about fiberglass gelcoat does not apply to it. Where it falls down for our customers is configurability: if you need a specific threshold height, a specific drain location, or a 58½-inch width, the acrylic market usually cannot serve it.

Strengths

  • Solid through the body; damage sands and buffs out completely
  • Dense and substantial underfoot, no drumming
  • Non-porous, very easy to keep clean
  • Premium appearance close to stone

Weak points

  • Expensive, often by a large margin
  • Heavy — a two-person carry and a floor-loading question upstairs
  • Limited barrier-free and accessible configurations
  • Fewer suppliers, longer lead times

If budget is genuinely not a constraint and accessibility is not the driver, solid surface is a lovely material. For a step-free shower in an existing house, it rarely wins on practicality.

Strengths

  • Convincing stone and tile appearance with no grout
  • Very fast install — a one-day wall is realistic
  • Moisture-resistant core behind the decorative face
  • Excellent choice for a wall-only refresh over a sound base

Weak points

  • Walls only. You still need a proper pan underneath
  • Edge detailing matters; a poorly sealed edge is the failure point
  • Stock sizes; fewer custom options than molded units
  • Not a structural substitute for a wood-backed panel unless specified

This is the Crestline category. It exists because tiling a remodel takes a week and a laminate wall takes a day, and from two feet away most people cannot tell the difference. Pair it with a molded pan and you get the look of tile without the part of tile that leaks.

Strengths

  • Any shape, any size, any pattern
  • Highest design ceiling of anything on this page
  • Individual tiles can be replaced
  • Strong buyer appeal in higher-end homes

Weak points

  • Grout is porous. It stains, it harbors mold, it needs sealing
  • The pan is the failure point, and it is field-built
  • Cost and schedule are several times a molded unit
  • Quality depends entirely on the installer, not the product
  • A curbless tiled shower needs a mortar bed and real skill

A well-built tiled shower is excellent and will outlast most of this list. A badly built one destroys a ceiling. The variance is the problem, and it is not something you can inspect from the finished surface. If you want tile, the sensible hybrid is a molded pan with tile on the walls above it.

The pattern worth noticing

Almost every "fixable" row is a surface problem. Almost every "not really" row is a structure problem. When you are comparing two kits, spend your money on the structure, because the surface can be maintained and the structure cannot.

Care

The habit that ruins every material on this page

Abrasive cleaning.

Scouring powder, a green scrub pad, a melamine sponge used hard, a bleach routine run weekly. Each of them takes microscopic material off the surface every time. On gelcoat you are removing the finish. On acrylic you are dulling the sheet. On grout you are opening the pores.

The surface then holds soap scum more readily, which makes people scrub harder, which accelerates the whole thing. That loop is the real reason showers look tired at year eight, far more often than any inherent defect in the material.

What to use instead

  • CleanerNon-abrasive liquid, pH-neutral
  • ToolSoft cloth or sponge, no grit
  • FrequencyLight and often beats hard and rarely
  • Hard waterSqueegee the walls; vinegar solution occasionally
  • NeverScouring powder, steel wool, stiff brushes
  • Gloss recoveryAutomotive polish restores gelcoat shine

A trick worth knowing

Gelcoat is the same material as a boat hull. When it loses its shine, the fix is the same one boat owners use: a fine automotive cutting polish and a soft pad. It comes back. Nothing brings back a scratched-through laminate.

Conditions

What a shower actually does to a material

Four environmental factors do all the damage. Which of them matters most depends on where you live and how the bathroom is used.

Hard water

Dissolved calcium and magnesium deposit on every surface as water evaporates. On gelcoat and acrylic that shows as a film that dulls the finish. Left long enough, the mineral layer etches into the surface and no amount of cleaning brings it back, because the surface itself has changed.

The fix is mechanical, not chemical. A squeegee on the walls after a shower removes the water before it can evaporate and deposit anything. It takes fifteen seconds and it is the single most effective maintenance habit for any shower surface. Failing that, an occasional dilute vinegar wash dissolves deposits before they set.

Thermal cycling

A shower surface goes from room temperature to hot and back, twice a day, for decades. Materials expand and contract at different rates, and anywhere two different materials meet, that movement has to go somewhere.

This is why joints matter more than faces. A sealed joint between a panel and a pan is working every single day. A locking mechanical joint that does not rely on a bead of silicone to carry the movement is structurally better, and it is one of the real differences between a well-engineered multi-piece system and a cheap one.

Standing water at edges

Every material on this page is fine with water on its face. The failures happen at edges: an unsealed laminate edge, a cut penetration for a valve that was never sealed properly, grout at a corner, the joint where a pan meets a wall.

When you inspect a shower, ignore the middle of the wall. Look at the corners, the bottom of the panels, and anywhere a hole was cut on site.

Cleaning chemistry

Covered above, but worth repeating in this context because it interacts with everything else. Bleach-heavy routines and abrasives roughen a surface at a microscopic level. A rougher surface holds mineral deposits and soap scum more readily. That makes the surface look dirtier sooner, which prompts more aggressive cleaning.

Most showers that look tired at year eight are not worn out. They are scratched, and the scratches are holding dirt.

What hard water means for your material choice

If you are in a hard-water area, darker and patterned finishes hide deposits far better than gloss white. That is not a durability difference, it is a visibility difference, but it changes how often you feel compelled to scrub — which is a durability difference after all.

Ventilation, which is nobody's favorite topic

An underpowered or unused extractor fan leaves a bathroom humid for hours after a shower. That extends the time every surface, joint and edge spends wet, and it is the main driver of mold on grout and of moisture reaching the core of a panel through a compromised edge.

A good fan, run for fifteen minutes after the shower, does more for the lifespan of a bathroom than most upgrades you can buy. It is also usually the cheapest item on the invoice.

The one-line version

If the shower has to be step-free, or built to a dimension, or hold a grab bar, reinforced fiberglass is the material that does all three. If none of those apply and you just want a nice surface in a standard opening, acrylic is excellent and widely available.

If someone tells you one material is simply better than the others with no qualification, they are selling that material. Including us.

Material questions

Is acrylic better than fiberglass for a shower?

Compared to a thin flat-pack fiberglass kit, yes, usually. Acrylic has color through the sheet and holds its gloss longer. Compared to a commercial-grade reinforced fiberglass unit with a heavy gelcoat and wood-backed walls, the comparison flips on everything that matters for accessibility: custom sizes, threshold heights, drain positions and grab bar mounting. The material name is not the deciding factor; the build is.

Does a gelcoat shower really only last seven years?

A thin one, cleaned with abrasives, plausibly. A heavy gelcoat over a barrier coat on a thick laminate is a different product and is commonly warranted for 30 years against manufacturing defects. Cleaning method matters more than age: scouring powder will dull any gelcoat in a couple of years, and a liquid cleaner and soft cloth will keep it glossy for decades.

Can a scratched shower be repaired?

Gelcoat, usually yes. Light scratches polish out with a fine automotive cutting compound, and deeper ones can be filled with gelcoat repair paste and polished. Solid surface sands out completely. Acrylic buffs. Laminate panels and tile are the ones where damage generally means replacing a piece.

Which material is best for a curbless shower?

A molded reinforced fiberglass pan, in almost every case. A half-inch threshold requires a precisely molded bevel, a factory slope to the drain and usually a trench. Field-built curbless tile showers can achieve it, but they require a mortar bed, a skilled installer and more floor depth than most retrofits have.

Do laminate wall panels hold grab bars?

Only if there is something structural behind them. A decorative laminate panel over drywall does not. If grab bars are part of the plan, either specify panels with laminated wood backing or add blocking in the framing at the exact positions before the panels go up. Deciding the positions in advance is the annoying part, and it is why backed panels are worth the money.

Is tile worth it?

For appearance and resale in a higher-end home, often yes. For reliability and cost, usually no. The compromise most people are happiest with is a molded pan with tile above it. The pan is the part that leaks and the part that has to be sloped correctly, so removing that risk while keeping the tile look is a genuinely good trade.
